General Motors · District Sales Manager · July 2021 to November 2023

General Motors: 48 dealerships, three districts, one job.

Role district sales managerScope 48 franchised dealerships across three districtsOutcome district ranking 136th to 66th of 185

the job

The title says sales. The work was closer to growth strategy for a portfolio of independent businesses. Each of my 48 franchised dealerships had its own owner, its own market, and its own version of "we're doing fine," and my job was to figure out where each one actually stood, what was holding it back, and how to get it to its goals. Over two and a half years I did that across three very different geographies, from the Mountain West to the central plains to the mid-South.

how i worked

Everything started with data: sales, market share, and operations numbers for every store, benchmarked against the district and the region. I built a performance tracking framework across all three markets so I could see regional demand and competitive dynamics in one place and turn them into go-to-market recommendations per store. Then the harder part: presenting those findings to dealer principals, owners who had been in the business for decades, and converting the analysis into plans they'd actually execute. I did that with more than 50 dealer principals and corporate leaders.

The stated problem was almost never the real problem. A store that thought it had a marketing gap usually had an execution gap, and no marketing budget fixes that.

one example

A rural dealership in one of my districts wanted to compete with the big metro stores on volume and retention, despite sitting far enough out that many customers would drive past it. The obvious answers were marketing or inventory. When I dug into their numbers, the gap was execution consistency: experienced salespeople skipping steps in the sales process, and a team that barely knew a company-wide loyalty program existed, even though it was exactly the tool a rural store needed to keep customers from drifting to the city.

So the first phase of their growth plan was entirely about capability. Full sales-process training for the whole team, veterans included. A specialist brought in to teach the loyalty program properly. Then individual sessions with every salesperson, going through their own metrics. Within the first year, that store's new vehicle revenue grew 7%, and the follow-up cadence we built kept customers coming back.

what it added up to

Across the districts, the ranking climbed from 136th to 66th out of 185 nationally. Revenue grew 20% and margin grew 9%. Dealer market share grew 5% and annual vehicle sales grew 12%. Those numbers are the sum of a lot of individual, unglamorous fixes like the one above.

what i learned

Before you can execute a growth strategy, you have to build the foundation underneath it, and sometimes that means going back to basics with people who think they already know everything. I also learned that a plan only counts if the people on the ground can run it, which is the thing I carried into business school and every project since.
