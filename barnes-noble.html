Why are we still walking into bookstores?

Role co-authorFormat case study and presentationPartner A.D. Schmidt

the question

By every structural measure of the last two decades, the physical bookstore was supposed to be dead. Amazon built a vertically integrated reading ecosystem with unlimited selection and algorithmic discovery. Borders went bankrupt in 2011. Barnes & Noble lost hundreds of millions on the Nook trying to fight back on Amazon's terms and cycled through CEOs. And yet, on a Friday night in a suburban mall, teenagers still wander into a Barnes & Noble, find a book, and sit down with a coffee to read it. We wanted to understand why.

the argument

Our case study argues that Barnes & Noble's recovery under James Daunt, who took over in 2019, was a deliberate retreat from platform competition and a return to what independent bookstores always did best: curate, contextualize, and build community around books. Daunt pushed inventory decisions down to store managers, ended publisher-paid placement that made stores feel like catalogs, and let each location develop its own identity. A handwritten staff pick on an index card is something an algorithm cannot produce, and it turns out people will drive to a store for it.

Amazon dominates commerce and consumption. It doesn't produce commentary. The bookstore's entire value is in that third thing.

why it matters beyond books

The pattern shows up everywhere value is subjective: fashion floors, record stores, art galleries. As the number of options grows, human curation becomes more valuable, not less. That's a useful lens for any business being told that a platform will eat it.
